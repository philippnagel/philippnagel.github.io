entsoe-cim

entsoe-cim

Aug 16, 20269 min read

Overview

ENTSO-E (European Network of Transmission System Operators for Electricity) coordinates transmission-level grid operation, cross-border capacity allocation, and balancing across ~40 European TSOs, using the IEC Common Information Model (CIM — IEC 61970/61968/62325) as its shared data language. Historically this layer stopped firmly at the TSO/wholesale boundary: retail-market harmonization (supplier switching, metering-data exchange between DSOs/suppliers/customers) was the job of a separate, much less institutionally powerful body, ebIX, which closed at the end of 2023 and handed its retail-facing role/data models to a new EU DSO Entity / ENTSO-E Joint Working Group. Since then, an EU-level binding rule — Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/1162 (effective 5 January 2025) — has, for the first time, set an EU-wide reference model for metering/consumption data access, built explicitly on the ebIX/HEMRM/CIM lineage. This is a real (if narrow and recent) crack in the “TSO layer doesn’t touch retail” boundary that the previous stub version of this note assumed.

Standards body & governance

Three distinct bodies operate at this layer, each with a different mandate:

  • ENTSO-E — an association of European TSOs (Germany’s members: 50Hertz, Amprion, TenneT, TransnetBW). It drafts network codes and technical CIM-based specifications (CGMES, ESMP) but does not itself have final regulatory authority.
  • ACER (Agency for the Cooperation of Energy Regulators) — reviews and can revise ENTSO-E’s draft network codes/guidelines before submitting them to the European Commission with a recommendation. Under the revised Regulation (EU) 2019/943, ACER has the explicit right to revise (not just comment on) ENTSO-E’s drafts. The Commission adopts final network codes/guidelines as implementing or delegated acts via comitology.
  • European Commission — sets the three-year priority list for new network codes, formally requests ACER (framework guidelines) and ENTSO-E (draft code text), and holds final adoption authority. It can develop a code unilaterally if ENTSO-E fails to deliver.

Retail-facing harmonization sat outside this chain entirely until recently. ebIX (European forum for energy Business Information eXchange, founded 2002, growing out of the 1993 Norwegian Statnett Marked EDIFACT implementation guides and the 1995 Ediel Nordic Forum) was an independent, EU-unrecognized non-profit that wrote Business Requirement Specifications (BRSs, ~38 of them) covering nearly all downstream processes: supplier switching, change of grid company, measured-data exchange, consent administration. Its member countries were Austria, Belgium, Finland, Germany, the Netherlands, Norway, Poland, Slovenia, and Sweden. ebIX formally closed at end of 2023, judging its role superseded by the newly EU-mandated EU DSO Entity (created under Regulation (EU) 2019/943) and the Joint Working Group between the EU DSO Entity and ENTSO-E, which has adopted joint work plans for 2024–2025 and carries statutory responsibilities under Articles 11–12 of Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/1162.

CIM’s own base standards (IEC 61970/61968/62325) are owned and maintained by IEC Technical Committee 57, working groups 13, 14, and 16 — a standards body independent of both ENTSO-E and the EU institutions, though ENTSO-E and (formerly) ebIX both fed change requests into it.

Protocol stack

Two genuinely separate stacks exist under the ENTSO-E/CIM umbrella, and conflating them was the main gap in the previous stub version of this note:

1. TSO/wholesale stack (mature, ENTSO-E-run):

  • IEC 61970 — power system/grid modeling (EMS, SCADA, network topology). ENTSO-E operationalized this as CGMES (Common Grid Model Exchange Specification), published 2013, mandated by EU regulators for TSO grid-model exchange from roughly 2015 onward.
  • IEC 61968 — distribution-side interoperability (asset/work management, customer information systems, metering) — the CIM branch that touches DSO-level systems, though it is a data-modeling standard for internal DSO IT integration, not a cross-border retail messaging mandate.
  • IEC 62325 — “Deregulated Market Communications.” Built on the 61970/61968 core object model, structured using UN/CEFACT’s Core Component Technical Specification (CCTS) methodology for deriving message profiles. Its European profile is the ESMP (European Style Market Profile), used for CACM (capacity allocation/congestion management), EBGL (balancing energy), and Transparency Platform reporting.

2. Retail/downstream stack (formerly ebIX, now JWG-owned):

  • ebIX’s own model was a UML model built with the UN/CEFACT Modelling Methodology (UMM) — a different modeling lineage than CIM’s UML, though both ultimately trace to UN/CEFACT conventions.
  • For roughly a decade before closure, ebIX ran a formal alignment project with IEC/TC57/WG16, submitting close to 100 Maintenance Requests since 2019 to get ebIX’s retail data elements represented in CIM, routed through an “ENTSO-E CIM for retail market work group” before final IEC approval. ebIX maintained its own CIM-derived layers: the European Style Downstream Market Profile (ESDMP) and various national CIM-based profiles.
  • ebIX’s BRSs were explicitly used as the specification basis for national retail data hubs in Belgium, Denmark, Finland, Norway, the Netherlands, Poland, Slovenia, and Sweden — i.e., most of its member countries did converge their retail data-hub design on ebIX/CIM-derived models.
  • Germany is the visible exception: it was an ebIX member but built its retail market communication independently on EDIFACT/EDI@Energy (see germany) rather than an ebIX/CIM-based data hub, and has no central datahub at all. Membership in the harmonization body did not force convergence — participation was voluntary and advisory, not legally binding, until the 2023/1162 regulation.

3. The new binding layer (2025 onward): Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2023/1162 (6 June 2023, effective 5 January 2025) establishes a mandatory EU-wide reference model — role model, information model, and process model — for access to metering and consumption data, covering roles like metered data administrator, metering point administrator, data access provider, and permission administrator. It explicitly reflects definitions and terminology from the ebIX-originated Harmonised Electricity Market Role Model (HEMRM) and IEC/CIM, and references IEC 62056 (DLMS/COSEM), EN 13757 (M-Bus), and other meter-facing standards for near-real-time data provision, while stating the reference model itself is “technology-neutral.” Member states had to report their national implementation practices to the Commission by 5 July 2025 for a public repository — this vault has not yet verified how far along that reporting/repository is as of August 2026, or whether Germany’s decentralized SMGW-gateway model is being treated as compliant.

Settlement & switching

Reassessing the stub’s “not applicable” claim: it was half right. There genuinely is no ENTSO-E/CIM-level retail supplier-switching process — that remains entirely national (Germany’s GPKE/WiM, etc.). But EBGL and CACM are real EU-level settlement regulations, not merely coordination frameworks: EBGL sets binding rules for balancing-energy settlement between TSOs (products, procurement, cross-border reserve sharing, and the settlement of balancing energy itself), and CACM governs capacity-allocation revenue distribution via implicit auctions in day-ahead/intraday coupled markets. These are TSO-to-TSO and TSO-to-market-operator settlement systems with legal force across the EU — a stronger claim than “harmonization layer above national markets,” which undersold it. The frontmatter above has been revised accordingly: settlement_model now distinguishes TSO-level settlement (genuinely EU-regulated) from retail settlement (still national). switching_process is revised similarly — no retail switching process exists here, but 2023/1162 does impose a binding EU data-access reference model that intersects with (without replacing) national switching processes.

Datahub / centralization

ENTSO-E operates the Transparency Platform (public data on generation, load, cross-border flows, outages) — a publication service, not a market-communication clearinghouse in the EDSN/DCC sense; it does not mediate transactional messages between market participants. At the retail layer, there is still no single EU datahub: national datahubs (where they exist, e.g. the Netherlands’ EDSN, several Nordic countries, Belgium) were built independently by each country, using ebIX BRSs as a common template rather than connecting to any shared European infrastructure. The 2023/1162 reference model does not create a central EU data hub either — it standardizes the model (roles, data objects, processes) that national data-access arrangements (centralized hub or bilateral) must expose, leaving the choice of centralized-vs-bilateral architecture to member states. Germany remains the clearest example of a member state implementing a fully decentralized (SMGW-gateway) model under this same EU umbrella.

Notable strengths / failure modes

Strength: the TSO/wholesale layer (CGMES, ESMP under CACM/EBGL) is a genuinely mature, mandatory, EU-regulated common data model with binding legal force via network codes/guidelines and real interoperability testing (ENTSO-E runs yearly CIM interoperability tests). This is a much stronger harmonization result than anything achieved at retail level.

Failure mode / structural gap: the retail-facing harmonization body (ebIX) was, for two decades, a voluntary, EU-unrecognized forum whose influence depended entirely on member goodwill — visible in Germany’s non-adoption of an ebIX-style datahub despite membership. Its 2023 closure created a real institutional risk (per ebIX’s own closure memo: loss of the UML model, UMM modeling knowledge, BRS maintenance capability, and CIM alignment expertise) that depended on a clean handover to the EU DSO Entity and Joint Working Group — a handover ebIX’s own memo flagged as not yet secured as of November 2023 (the EU DSO Entity had, at that point, declined to formally join the ebIX/EFET/ENTSO-E Harmonisation Group maintaining the HEMRM). Update (2026-08-16 pass): the gap is still not formally closed — the EU DSO Entity’s own 2026 work programme describes an “updated MoU between ENTSO-E and DSO Entity” as still “currently under development,” meaning the handover ebIX flagged in 2023 had not been formalized even three years later. No source found states exactly which expertise was or wasn’t absorbed in the interim.

Open structural question resolved: yes, this layer now does constrain (a slice of) national retail-market protocols — but only since January 2025, only for metering/consumption data access (not full switching-message formats), and via a route (ebIX → HEMRM → 2023/1162) that is organizationally distinct from ENTSO-E’s own CIM/62325 work, even though both ultimately reference the same IEC CIM base model.

Open questions

  • Current maturity of 2023/1162 national-practice reporting — partially resolved 2026-08-16: Article 10(4) confirms the 5 July 2025 deadline exactly as stated. Whether the resulting public repository is now live, and how complete member-state reporting actually is, remains not found — still open.
  • Whether the JWG has fully absorbed ebIX’s UMM/BRS expertise — resolved 2026-08-16 as still open, formally: an updated ENTSO-E/DSO Entity MoU is “currently under development” per the DSO Entity’s 2026 work programme, meaning the handover gap ebIX flagged in Nov 2023 hadn’t been formalized three years later.
  • CEEDS’s binding force and relationship to 2023/1162 — resolved 2026-08-16: CEEDS is not independently binding — the 3 June 2026 Strategic Roadmap anchors it as a coordination/architecture framework riding on already-binding regulation (2023/1162 plus upcoming supplier-switching/demand-response acts), not a new binding instrument itself.
  • CGMES/ESMP vs. retail reference model technical bridge — checked again 2026-08-16, genuinely not found (not just under-researched): no concrete technical bridge specification exists anywhere findable, only conceptual/terminology alignment via shared HEMRM vocabulary.
  • Whether/how the Netherlands’ EDSN (built on ebIX BRSs, confirmed — see netherlands) and the Nordics’ datahubs differ in their degree of CIM-native vs. CIM-adjacent implementation — flagged for the Netherlands and Nordic market notes, not resolved here.
  • ACER’s own website returned 404s — resolved 2026-08-16: acer.europa.eu is now live and directly confirms ACER’s 6-month network-code review/“reasoned opinion” role under Regulation 2019/943, matching the note’s existing secondary-sourced claim (now primary-sourced).
